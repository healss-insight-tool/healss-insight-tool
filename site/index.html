Measured literacy × self-reported confidence · U.S. adults

Who feels sure they can read what they sign, and who, as a group, likely cannot?

This project places two free national surveys side by side. One tests how well adults actually read. The other asks how confident they feel handling health information. The distance between the two, for the same policy-relevant groups, is the finding.

Adults 16–65 at or below Level 1 literacy
28%±2.0
PIAAC Cycle 2, 2023 · measured by test · reproduced here as 27.6%, 95% CI 25.6–29.6
Adults 18+ “very confident” filling out medical forms alone
65%±1.9
HINTS 7, 2024 · self-report · 65.3%, 95% CI 63.4–67.2

These are two population estimates from two independent samples, never the same people. The study compares groups to groups and never claims that any individual cannot read.

Why it matters

Medicaid signature pages read at an 11th to 18th grade level, with a mean near 15.5. Ninety-two percent of state renewal forms are written at or above a fifth-grade level, and in seven states a child's risk of losing coverage rose about 47% for each grade level added to the form. Research consent forms are no better: 91% of 5,239 forms on ClinicalTrials.gov sit above an eighth-grade level. Meanwhile the field increasingly measures health literacy by asking people how confident they feel rather than testing what they can do.

Where confidence runs far ahead of measured skill, three things follow. “I have read and understood” attestations may not reflect understanding. Coverage is lost for paperwork reasons rather than ineligibility, which is measurable administrative waste. And confident readers with low measured skill may be less likely to question official-looking mail, which is where scam warnings need to land.

What the project does

  1. Competence. From PIAAC, the share of each subgroup at or below Level 1 literacy and at Level 3 or above.
  2. Confidence. From HINTS, the share of the same subgroups reporting high confidence filling out medical forms.
  3. The gap. Confidence minus competence, in percentage points, with a confidence interval, for each subgroup.
  4. Channels. Where the highest-gap groups get health information and whom they trust, so notices and warnings reach them.

No new data are collected. Both files are public and de-identified. The analysis is preregistered before any outcome data are examined, and every figure is regenerated from the official downloads by script.

Study design

Two surveys, one gap

Each quantity is estimated inside its own survey, for identically defined subgroups, and the results are compared side by side. The surveys are never linked at the individual level. The unit of comparison is the subgroup, never the person.

Competence · PIAAC Cycle 2 (2023)Directly measured literacy on a 0–500 scale, reported as ten plausible values per person. Primary threshold: at or below Level 1 (score below 226). Sensitivity: below Level 3 (below 276). Sample: 3,765 U.S. adults 16–65.
Confidence · HINTS 7 (2024)“How confident are you filling out medical forms by yourself?” High confidence = “Very.” Secondary item: “I have the skills to find the health information I need.” Sample: 7,278 U.S. adults 18 and older.

Research questions

  1. What share of each subgroup scores at or below Level 1 and at Level 3 or above?
  2. What share of the same subgroups reports high confidence handling health information?
  3. For which subgroups does confidence most exceed measured competence?
  4. Among the highest-gap groups, which information sources do people use and trust? (exploratory)

Hypotheses

  • H1. Adults without a high school diploma, as a group, score lower on measured literacy than adults with more education. (Version 1 uses education as the primary axis; the Medicaid comparison originally planned here waits for restricted data, see below.)
  • H2. Self-reported confidence varies less across subgroups than measured literacy does, so the gap is widest among adults without a high school diploma.
  • H2-alt. Because more-educated people overestimate their health literacy more often, the gap may also be substantial among higher-education groups. Both are stated so either result is publishable.

Estimation

Every estimate uses its own survey's official weights and variance method: 50 jackknife replicates for HINTS, 44 Fay-adjusted BRR replicates combined across 10 plausible values for PIAAC. The gap for a subgroup is confidence minus competence; because the samples are independent, its variance is the sum of the two. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals throughout. Sensitivity analyses: the Level 3 threshold, the secondary confidence item, the strictest common age range, and HINTS response mode (paper versus web).

Guardrails

  • Write “adults without a diploma, as a group, score X in PIAAC and report Y in HINTS,” never claims about the same people.
  • No translation of PIAAC levels into school grade levels without a published, justified crosswalk.
  • The study does not measure fraud. Fraud, waste and abuse claims describe vulnerability pathways and administrative waste.
  • No claim that low literacy causes fraud or raises medical costs; the evidence on both is mixed.
  • Statistical matching across surveys is out of scope for version 1.

Weights and variance

HINTS 7. Point estimates use the full-sample weight PERSON_FINWT0. Variance uses the 50 jackknife replicate weights PERSON_FINWT1–50 with the coefficient NCI specifies (0.98, which is 49/50):

V(θ̂) = 0.98 · Σr=1..50 (θ̂r − θ̂)²

PIAAC Cycle 2, U.S. Point estimates use SPFWT0. The U.S. file uses Fay-adjusted balanced repeated replication with 44 replicates (SPFWT1–44) and Fay's factor k = 0.3. Columns 45 to 80 are copies of the full-sample weight and are excluded.

Vrep(θ̂) = Σr=1..44 (θ̂r − θ̂)² / (44 · (1 − 0.3)²)

Literacy is reported as ten plausible values per person. Each statistic is computed once per plausible value and combined with Rubin's rules, where Ū is the mean replicate variance and B the variance of the ten point estimates:

θ̂ = mean(θ̂m)    V = Ū + (1 + 1/10) · B

Proficiency levels (OECD 2023 Data Analysis Manual, Table 5.2): below Level 1 < 176 · Level 1 176–225 · Level 2 226–275 · Level 3 276–325 · Level 4 326–375 · Level 5 ≥ 376. "At or below Level 1" means a score below 226.

The gap. For subgroup g, confidence minus competence in percentage points. The samples are independent, so the variances add:

Gapg = Cg − Pg    SE(Gapg) = √(V(Cg) + V(Pg))

All intervals on this site are 95% intervals, estimate ± 1.96 × SE.

Reproduce a figure

With the archived files in place, Step 0 regenerates its whole comparison table in one command. The same module serves every later chart.

cd analysis/step0
python3 step0_reproduce.py        # writes step0_results.csv and step0_results.md

import survey_est as se
